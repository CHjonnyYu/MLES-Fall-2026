Warpage and thermal design

Compare process-stage deformation and thermal performance; test how one property change affects a decision and whether public measurements support the model.

How this week works: Three approximately 56-minute modules plus two short transitions. Each hour has a distinct teaching target and a reviewable evidence checkpoint. InPack.AI is the course analysis platform; the handout records assumptions and outputs without inventing a software feature.

Public case: Published fan-out warpage simulation and experiment

The IEEE paper follows fan-out warpage through manufacturing stages and reports agreement and a thin-wafer discrepancy. Students extract the stage, geometry, reference measurement, and model limitation before adapting the method to a course case.

Evidence boundary: The paper's measured agreement is specific to its own specimen and setup. The slides never present those values as InPack.AI output.

Teaching modules

H1 · Warpage is a process history

Outcome: Track deformation across operations instead of treating one final shape as the whole reliability story.

Evidence to keep: A stage-by-stage warpage record with temperature, support state, and sign convention.

Open H1 slidesSource notes

Choose a displacement definition

Peak-to-valley, center-to-edge, and curvature are different summaries; specify sign and reference plane.

Worked reasoning: A synthetic +80 µm center-to-edge value and 80 µm peak-to-valley value need not describe the same shape.

Boundary: The 80 µm values are synthetic teaching data.

Check your understanding: Can two 80 µm labels be treated as identical?

No. First match their definitions, reference, direction, and temperature.

Sources: R6 R8

Stress-free state matters

Cure and cooling history determine the reference from which thermal mismatch creates strain.

Worked reasoning: Model a molded panel after cure and after room-temperature debond. Identify what support or stress-free temperature may change between stages.

Check your understanding: Will a single room-temperature material table capture the history?

Not necessarily. Process state and temperature-dependent properties must be represented.

Sources: R7 R8

Handling exposes intermediate risk

A panel can violate a handling or alignment limit at an intermediate step while appearing acceptable at final inspection.

Worked reasoning: The public fan-out study explicitly examines stage changes. Ask which stage a gripper or alignment tool sees, not just the final package.

Check your understanding: Does final flatness clear a prior handling failure?

No. Evaluate the geometry at the relevant operation and support condition.

Sources: R8 R9

Measurement fixtures affect thin parts

Gravity, support, and instrument setup can change an observed deformation of a thin wafer or panel.

Worked reasoning: The published study notes a thin-wafer discrepancy. Separate possible model deficiency from fixture effects before calibrating a material value.

Check your understanding: Can every discrepancy be fixed by tuning CTE?

No. Check measurement setup, geometry, boundary conditions, and model form.

Sources: R8

H2 · Sensitivity and thermal tradeoffs

Outcome: Change one input, interpret response, and refuse a ranking when conditions are not matched.

Evidence to keep: A one-factor sensitivity table with baseline, variation, output change, and validity limit.

Open H2 slidesSource notes

One-factor change isolates a question

A controlled sensitivity run changes one declared parameter while retaining geometry, stage, loads, boundaries, and output definition.

Worked reasoning: Synthetic exercise: vary EMC CTE by ±10% around a stated baseline and record warpage at the same process stage.

Boundary: The ±10% perturbation is a synthetic classroom choice.

Check your understanding: Does a sensitivity slope prove the true CTE?

No. It describes the model's local response around the assumed input.

Sources: R6 R8

A response can be nonlinear

Warpage or temperature may change direction or slope near a material transition or structural instability.

Worked reasoning: Compare property behavior below and above an EMC transition. Do not extrapolate a local slope across a new phase or cure state.

Check your understanding: May one linear derivative cover all temperatures?

Only if the range and model justify that approximation.

Sources: R7 R9

Thermal improvements have costs

Changing an interface or stack may reduce thermal resistance while increasing stiffness mismatch or routing difficulty.

Worked reasoning: A thicker copper spreader may aid heat flow but alter package bending; rerun warpage and check assembly clearance.

Check your understanding: Does lower junction temperature settle the package choice?

No. Check hard mechanical and electrical constraints too.

Sources: R2 R4 R7

Use a reversal test

A useful sensitivity study asks whether plausible input uncertainty can reverse the candidate decision.

Worked reasoning: If ranking flips over the measured conductivity range, withhold the recommendation until that property or a package response is measured.

Check your understanding: When is a small output change still important?

When it crosses a hard limit or reverses the selection.

Sources: R6

H3 · Compare prediction with measurement

Outcome: Build matched pairs, inspect residuals, and write a limited validation claim.

Evidence to keep: A comparison table with specimen, stage, temperature, metric, residual, and next test.

Open H3 slidesSource notes

Make a matched pair

Prediction and measurement must describe the same specimen definition, process stage, temperature, support condition, and warpage metric.

Worked reasoning: Reject a comparison if the model is after grinding but the measured value is after debonding; find the correct record first.

Check your understanding: Should the mismatch be averaged into an error metric?

No. It is not a valid pair for that metric.

Sources: R6 R8

Residuals reveal pattern

Errors by process stage can expose a model limitation hidden by one favorable comparison.

Worked reasoning: Chen et al. report simulation/experiment warpage pairs of 0.172/0.160, 1.473/0.887, and 1.022/0.620 mm. Calculate approximate relative errors of 7.5%, 66.1%, and 64.8% against the measured values.

Boundary: Published values belong to Chen et al.'s specimen; they are not InPack.AI outputs.

Check your understanding: Does the close PI2 result validate all later stages?

No. The larger molding and grinding residuals must be reported and investigated.

Sources: R6 R13

Report the tested domain

Validation evidence supports only the geometry, materials, stages, and ranges actually tested.

Worked reasoning: The published fan-out study's thin-wafer caveat becomes a boundary in the credibility card rather than a footnote to omit.

Check your understanding: Can its reported agreement be generalized to every package?

No. Describe its domain and failure region.

Sources: R6 R8

Choose the next physical check

Target a measurement that distinguishes the leading explanations for the residual.

Worked reasoning: If the residual grows only after debond, compare support/fixture conditions before changing all material parameters.

Check your understanding: Why not tune every input until the curve fits?

That can hide wrong mechanisms and destroys an independent validation test.
