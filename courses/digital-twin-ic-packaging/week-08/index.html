Thermal, mechanical, and electrical co-design

Trace heat, deformation, and electrical return paths through one package; evaluate coupled tradeoffs rather than ranking a single maximum.

How this week works: Three approximately 56-minute modules plus two short transitions. Each hour has a distinct teaching target and a reviewable evidence checkpoint. InPack.AI is the course analysis platform; the handout records assumptions and outputs without inventing a software feature.

Public case: Published fan-out warpage study

The IEEE study models warpage across debonding, grinding, and annealing and compares simulations with physical measurements. Its abstract reports close agreement for much of its studied setup and a substantial discrepancy for very thin wafers.

Evidence boundary: Its reported agreement is conditional on that geometry, process, model, and measurement setup; it does not establish accuracy for a classroom package or InPack.AI.

Teaching modules

H1 · Follow the heat path

Outcome: Estimate where power becomes temperature rise and where a thermal boundary enters the model.

Evidence to keep: A labeled heat-path sketch with assumptions, units, and one candidate measurement.

Open H1 slidesSource notes

Power creates a temperature gradient

Steady heat flow needs both a source and a route to a cooler boundary; peak power alone does not define junction temperature.

Worked reasoning: Synthetic check: at 10 W, a 2 K/W path implies about 20 K rise in a lumped steady-state estimate; that estimate excludes local hot spots.

Boundary: The 10 W and 2 K/W values are synthetic classroom numbers.

Check your understanding: Does the 20 K estimate predict every die location?

No. It is a lumped estimate under stated power and boundary conditions, not a spatial map.

Sources: R4

Interfaces can dominate resistance

A small contact area or poorly characterized thermal interface can bottleneck an otherwise conductive stack.

Worked reasoning: Compare two synthetic stacks with the same die and sink but different interface contact quality. State what thickness, conductivity, pressure, and voiding data are missing.

Check your understanding: Can bulk conductivity alone rank the two stacks?

No. Contact resistance and the actual heat-flow geometry matter.

Sources: R4

Parallel paths share heat

Some heat can flow upward through a lid while some flows downward through joints and the board; the shares depend on resistance and boundaries.

Worked reasoning: Trace both paths for a flip-chip package. If the top sink is removed, predict a redistribution of heat before looking at a model.

Check your understanding: Does removing a top sink leave bottom heat flow unchanged?

Usually not. Boundary changes can alter the entire temperature field and flux distribution.

Sources: R4 R2

State what temperature means

Junction peak, case temperature, board temperature, and transient versus steady values answer different questions.

Worked reasoning: A synthetic report lists 85 °C at the case at 5 W and 90 °C junction at 10 W. Refuse a direct material comparison until load and location match.

Boundary: Temperatures and loads are synthetic teaching values.

Check your understanding: Which metadata must accompany a reported temperature?

Location, load, boundary condition, time or process stage, units, and measurement or model origin.

Sources: R4 R6

H2 · CTE mismatch and joint reliability

Outcome: Explain why a package bends and how repeated strain can affect an interconnect.

Evidence to keep: A warpage and joint-risk map across at least two temperatures or process stages.

Open H2 slidesSource notes

Differential expansion drives curvature

Layers with different coefficients of thermal expansion and stiffness cannot expand freely when bonded; bending can result.

Worked reasoning: Sketch silicon on an organic substrate as temperature changes. Identify which layer tends to expand more; do not infer curvature sign without the actual stack and stress-free state.

Check your understanding: Can CTE difference alone determine warpage sign?

No. Layer order, thickness, modulus, cure history, and reference temperature also matter.

Sources: R7 R8

Material properties vary with temperature

A single room-temperature modulus may misrepresent behavior through reflow, cure, or service cycling.

Worked reasoning: Compare an epoxy molding compound below and above its glass-transition region using its measured property curve rather than one constant value.

Check your understanding: Why is one catalog number insufficient?

Because CTE and stiffness can change with temperature and cure state; the process path matters.

Sources: R7 R8

Warpage is stage-dependent

The shape after molding, debonding, grinding, and annealing need not rank designs in the same order.

Worked reasoning: The published fan-out study tracks multiple manufacturing stages. Ask whether a room-temperature final shape can explain a failure during an earlier assembly step.

Check your understanding: Is final-state flatness enough to clear process risk?

No. An intermediate stage can exceed handling or alignment limits.

Sources: R8 R9

Joint strain needs local geometry

Global warpage can signal risk, but joint fatigue depends on local displacement, geometry, material behavior, and cycling.

Worked reasoning: Mark an outer-corner bump and a central bump in a package sketch. Predict which sees a larger relative displacement under one idealized thermal cycle, then name missing data.

Check your understanding: Does a warpage maximum equal a fatigue lifetime?

No. Lifetime requires a suitable local damage model and validation data.

Sources: R7

H3 · Electrical paths and tradeoff review

Outcome: Connect signal integrity and power integrity to thermal and mechanical changes without overclaiming one metric.

Evidence to keep: A two-candidate table of objectives, hard constraints, measurements, and unresolved risk.

Open H3 slidesSource notes

A signal needs a reference

A package trace and its return path form an electrical loop; geometry influences impedance and noise.

Worked reasoning: On a proposed RDL route, mark the nearest reference path. A layer transition that breaks continuity deserves an explicit check.

Check your understanding: Can a short trace be assumed electrically safe?

No. Discontinuity, return path, coupling, and load conditions also matter.

Sources: R2 R3

Power delivery has impedance

The package power network affects voltage at the die during changing current, and resistive loss also produces heat.

Worked reasoning: A candidate narrows a supply route to ease signal escape. Ask which voltage-drop and thermal checks must be repeated.

Check your understanding: Does routing relief guarantee a better design?

No. Power integrity and local heating may worsen; assess both under the same load.

Sources: R2 R3 R4

Compare only compatible outputs

A lower peak temperature may coexist with greater warpage or a routing-rule violation; hard constraints and objectives must be separated.

Worked reasoning: Synthetic A is 2 K cooler but fails an assumed routing rule; B meets the rule with a higher yet acceptable temperature. Reject A under that hard rule.

Boundary: The 2 K comparison is synthetic teaching data.

Check your understanding: Which candidate wins if a hard rule is violated?

Do not rank solely by the temperature; resolve the rule violation first.

Sources: R2 R6

Choose a discriminating measurement

The next test should target the uncertainty most likely to reverse the design decision.

Worked reasoning: If interface resistance dominates the thermal ranking, specify a test coupon, heat load, sensor location, and comparison temperature.

Check your understanding: Why is another simulation run not always the next best step?

It may repeat the same uncertain input; a physical measurement can reduce the uncertainty that matters.
