Substrate and interposer design

Move from I/O assignment to a physically feasible routing plan across RDL, vias, substrate, and interposer interfaces.

How this week works: Three approximately 56-minute modules plus two short transitions. Each hour has a distinct teaching target and a reviewable evidence checkpoint. InPack.AI is the course analysis platform; the handout records assumptions and outputs without inventing a software feature.

Public case: IEEE interconnect roadmap and UCIe specifications

The IEEE roadmap describes density and physical constraints in 2D and 3D interconnects. UCIe provides a public interface specification context for chiplet links; students use these sources to build a traceable constraint checklist.

Evidence boundary: A public interface standard or roadmap does not certify that a particular student layout is routed, compliant, or manufacturable.

Teaching modules

H1 · Map interfaces and I/O

Outcome: Separate logical links from physical pads, bumps, vias, and package balls.

Evidence to keep: An I/O assignment map with power, ground, signal, test, and spare categories.

Open H1 slidesSource notes

Follow the complete connection

A chiplet-to-chiplet signal may cross bumps, RDL or interposer wiring, another bump field, and return paths.

Worked reasoning: Trace one data lane from a compute chiplet to memory and mark each physical interface where loss or failure can occur.

Check your understanding: Is a logical link diagram enough to assess physical risk?

No. It omits pitch, layer transitions, materials, and return-path geometry.

Sources: R2 R3

Reserve non-signal contacts

Power, ground, clock, test, spare, and thermal structures consume area and constrain placement.

Worked reasoning: Synthetic assignment: 600 signal bumps plus 200 power/ground and 24 test gives 824 assigned sites, before additional margin.

Boundary: Contact counts are synthetic teaching data.

Check your understanding: Why not route all 824 as data?

Their functions and placement rules differ; the raw total is not signal capacity.

Sources: R3 R11

Pitch sets an escape problem

Bump pitch and pad arrangement determine whether inner rows can reach legal routing channels.

Worked reasoning: Sketch an inner bump row. Name one legal via route and one keep-out before asserting that the link is feasible.

Check your understanding: Can more metal layers always fix poor assignment?

Not necessarily. Via space, yield, cost, and assembly rules may still block it.

Sources: R3

Record interface standards carefully

A specification gives definitions and requirements; actual compliance needs matching implementation details and tests.

Worked reasoning: Use the UCIe public specification page to name the version referenced in a design brief; do not claim compliance from a block label.

Check your understanding: Does 'UCIe-compatible' follow from naming UCIe?

No. Check the applicable version, required electrical and protocol behavior, and conformance evidence.

Sources: R11

H2 · Routing rules and stackup

Outcome: Check line and space, layer transitions, vias, reference planes, and manufacturing constraints.

Evidence to keep: A routing sketch with stated rules and one failed constraint repaired.

Open H2 slidesSource notes

Track capacity is finite

Route count across a corridor depends on legal line width, spacing, keep-outs, and layer count.

Worked reasoning: Synthetic corridor: 14 required traces but 10 legal tracks on one layer; change assignment, placement, or stackup and recalculate.

Boundary: Track counts are synthetic teaching data.

Check your understanding: May a route sketch ignore the four missing tracks?

No. It must record the violation and a legal redesign.

Sources: R2 R3

Vias cost area and continuity

A layer transition occupies keep-out space and can change signal and return-path behavior.

Worked reasoning: Mark the signal via and nearby reference transition in a two-layer escape sketch. If the return path detours, flag it for SI review.

Check your understanding: Is the shortest signal trace always best?

No. The return path and transition discontinuity matter.

Sources: R2 R3

Interposer and substrate differ

A silicon interposer and an organic substrate differ in achievable feature scale, material properties, cost, and assembly roles.

Worked reasoning: A dense chiplet link may require fine interposer routing, while package balls and board connection remain at a larger scale; map both levels.

Check your understanding: Can one pitch number describe the entire package?

No. Each interface and manufacturing layer has its own design rules.

Sources: R1 R3

Power and signal compete for space

A routing choice that helps one net may weaken a power path or remove reference continuity.

Worked reasoning: Move an RDL lane through a power region. Mark the power discontinuity and the measurement or simulation needed to assess it.

Check your understanding: Does a routed netlist establish power integrity?

No. Current demand, impedance, return paths, and decoupling still need evaluation.

Sources: R2 R3

H3 · Select between routing candidates

Outcome: Use hard rules, electrical behavior, process constraints, and traceable evidence to choose a route.

Evidence to keep: A candidate comparison with a legal route, unresolved risk, and next verification step.

Open H3 slidesSource notes

Screen hard constraints first

A layout that violates width, space, via, or assembly rules cannot be ranked as ready by a softer performance metric.

Worked reasoning: Synthetic A is shorter but crosses a forbidden keep-out; B is longer and legal. Record A as requiring redesign before SI ranking.

Boundary: The route comparison is synthetic teaching data.

Check your understanding: Should A win because its route is shorter?

No. Resolve the hard violation before comparing performance.

Sources: R2 R3

Compare under common conditions

Signal loss, temperature, voltage drop, and cost must use matched geometry definitions, operating conditions, and units.

Worked reasoning: If one candidate reports loss at a different frequency, do not place the two numbers in a single rank column.

Check your understanding: What is the first action when the frequency differs?

Recompute or acquire matched-condition results, then rank.

Sources: R2 R6

Inspect failure access

Testability and inspection access belong in the design brief before manufacturing, especially for buried connections.

Worked reasoning: For an interposer-connected chiplet, specify what an electrical open would show and which imaging or destructive analysis could localize it.

Check your understanding: Does a final electrical pass prove a sound joint?

No. Some latent physical damage can remain undetected under that test condition.

Sources: R12 R3

Document the chosen route

The selection record includes source, rule version, route revision, model output definition, uncertainty, and a reviewer-visible reason.

Worked reasoning: A peer redraws one path and verifies every claimed rule from the brief. Any unverified rule remains open.

Check your understanding: When is the design ready for signoff?

Only after required constraints and evidence gates pass for the intended use.
