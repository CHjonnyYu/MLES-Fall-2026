Package design methods

Translate requirements into a chiplet floorplan, I/O map, stackup, routing constraints, and an evidence-backed design brief.

How this week works: Three approximately 56-minute modules plus two short transitions. Each hour has a distinct teaching target and a reviewable evidence checkpoint. InPack.AI is the course analysis platform; the handout records assumptions and outputs without inventing a software feature.

Public case: Public co-design case

The IEEE roadmap describes early chip, package, and board co-design, including floorplan and bump-map decisions. Use its design flow as a public reference; the classroom two-chip-plus-memory numbers are synthetic.

Evidence boundary: The roadmap is a technology assessment, not a measurement of our classroom candidate or an InPack.AI validation.

Teaching modules

H1 · Requirements become package constraints

Outcome: Convert a system request into quantities, interfaces, and checks before drawing a package.

Evidence to keep: A requirement table with measurable targets, owners, and missing values.

Open H1 slidesSource notes

Start with the system use case

Bandwidth, power, size, cost, and reliability compete; a package choice only makes sense against stated targets.

Worked reasoning: Synthetic example: a compute die beside four memory stacks needs a stated bandwidth and power budget before interposer size can be judged.

Check your understanding: Is 'high bandwidth' a design requirement?

No. State an interface, target quantity, units, operating condition, and acceptance rule.

Sources: R2

Budget the I/O before placement

Count signal, power, ground, test, and spare contacts separately; a total pin count hides different physical demands.

Worked reasoning: Synthetic worksheet: 800 signal connections plus 240 power/ground and 40 test contacts give 1080 assigned contacts, before routing margin.

Boundary: The 1080-contact arithmetic is synthetic teaching data.

Check your understanding: Can 1080 contacts be treated as 1080 equivalent signal routes?

No. Power, ground, test, and high-speed signals impose different placement and return-path constraints.

Sources: R2 R3

Floorplan near the data path

Adjacency shortens some routes but may concentrate heat and make escape routing harder.

Worked reasoning: Compare a close memory ring with a wider placement. The close layout may ease links while creating a hotter central region; neither wins without constraints.

Check your understanding: Which layout is better from distance alone?

Neither. Check link length, thermal behavior, I/O escape, power delivery, and manufacturing limits together.

Sources: R2 R10

Define the stackup explicitly

A stackup names each layer, material, interface, and thermal path; a cross-section is a design input, not decoration.

Worked reasoning: Trace one signal and one heat path through a proposed 2.5D stack. Mark every unknown thickness or conductivity rather than inventing it.

Check your understanding: Does a top-view floorplan specify the thermal path?

No. The vertical materials and interfaces must also be described.

Sources: R2 R4

H2 · Routing, power, and manufacturability

Outcome: Test whether the first floorplan can be routed and built under stated design rules.

Evidence to keep: A constraint checklist with one route sketch and at least one unresolved rule.

Open H2 slidesSource notes

RDL routes have width and spacing

Line width, spacing, layer count, and via rules bound how many nets can cross a channel.

Worked reasoning: Synthetic check: if a channel needs 12 tracks but a chosen rule allows only 9, revise layer count or placement; do not waive the rule silently.

Boundary: Track counts are synthetic teaching data.

Check your understanding: What does a congested channel force you to change?

Placement, assignment, stackup, or technology; rerun the constraint check after the change.

Sources: R2 R3

Escape routing starts at the bump map

A dense bump field must reach legal routing channels; logical connectivity alone does not prove physical escape.

Worked reasoning: Sketch inner-row bump escape for a chiplet. If two required routes cross on one layer, add a legal layer transition or change the bump assignment.

Check your understanding: Can a perfect netlist still be unroutable?

Yes. Pitch, layer rules, via keep-outs, and congestion can block physical implementation.

Sources: R2 R3

Power needs a return path

Signals, power, and ground must be planned together because current loops and impedance depend on physical geometry.

Worked reasoning: On the floorplan, draw a high-speed signal beside its reference return and then mark where the power path enters the compute die.

Check your understanding: What is missing if only signal lines are drawn?

The power and reference paths, plus their electrical and thermal consequences.

Sources: R2 R3

Apply design-for-manufacturing gates

Every candidate needs explicit limits for pitch, alignment, material compatibility, assembly sequence, and inspection access.

Worked reasoning: A candidate passes a routing sketch but violates an assumed bump pitch. Record the failed rule and revise before comparing simulated temperature.

Check your understanding: Should a thermally promising candidate survive a failed process rule?

No. A failed hard manufacturing constraint blocks that candidate until redesigned or the rule is verified.

Sources: R2 R3

H3 · Design brief and InPack.AI case audit

Outcome: Record two candidates, compare only like-for-like quantities, and defend the next check.

Evidence to keep: A one-page design brief with source, candidate assumptions, decision rule, uncertainty, and validation plan.

Open H3 slidesSource notes

Record a source before a result

A public architecture reference and a classroom model output are different evidence types; keep their provenance separate.

Worked reasoning: For the roadmap case, cite its chapter and page. For any InPack.AI demonstration, record the actual case ID and export revision; write 'not available' if absent.

Check your understanding: Can the roadmap's conclusion stand in for our model output?

No. It motivates a workflow but does not provide our candidate's result or accuracy.

Sources: R2 R6

Compare candidates on the same basis

Hold use case, load, temperature, units, and output definition fixed before ranking layouts.

Worked reasoning: Synthetic A and B differ only in memory placement. If A's temperature is reported at full power and B's at idle, refuse the comparison.

Boundary: Candidate A/B is a synthetic teaching scenario, not InPack.AI output.

Check your understanding: Which is the first action when conditions differ?

Normalize or rerun under matched conditions; do not rank the mismatched numbers.

Sources: R2 R6

Separate prediction from measurement

A predicted peak or warpage remains a model result until checked against a suitable physical measurement.

Worked reasoning: The public roadmap suggests co-design; a course run may predict a thermal difference. The design brief names what sensor, specimen, and condition would test it.

Check your understanding: Does agreement between two simulation runs validate the package?

No. It checks computational consistency, not physical accuracy.

Sources: R2 R6

Write a bounded recommendation

A recommendation should name the winning criterion, violated or unknown constraints, and the smallest useful next test.

Worked reasoning: Choose Candidate B only if it satisfies the declared constraints. State which material property or measurement could reverse the choice.

Check your understanding: What makes the recommendation reviewable?

Traceable inputs, a stated decision rule, comparable evidence, uncertainty, and a test that could falsify the claim.

Sources: R2 R6
